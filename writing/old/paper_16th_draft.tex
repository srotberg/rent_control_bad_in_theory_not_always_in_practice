\begin{abstract}
\onehalfspacing

\noindent Most economists oppose rent control for reducing supply, misallocating housing, distorting labor markets, and increasing evictions. Proponents argue it improves affordability by redistributing from landlords to low-income renters more effectively than government. We develop a general equilibrium model with supply responsive to rent control design and imperfect redistribution. In our Toronto-calibrated model, capping rents at the unit level — even after turnover — improves welfare relative to decontrol with tax cuts. But capping rents only for incumbents does not. The primary channel for welfare gains, however, is not redistribution, but rather curbing housing over-consumption driven by binding minimum rental size constraints.

\\[1.5ex]

\noindent \textbf{JEL Classification:} E17; R31; R52. 

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Rent control, general equilibrium, housing, tenancy control, unit control, homelessness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rent control is one of the most widely implemented — and hotly debated — housing policies worldwide, typically motivated by the goal of improving housing affordability for low-income renters.

Economists have long opposed rent control, arguing that it discourages rental housing supply, misallocates units, and generates deadweight loss \citep{gl-2003}. In standard frictionless models with perfectly elastic long-run supply and efficient lump-sum redistribution, rent control is unequivocally welfare-reducing. With perfectly elastic supply, any binding price ceiling pushes rents below the break-even level, eliminating supply and harming even the poorest renters. With efficient redistribution, the government could tax landlord profits in a lump-sum fashion and transfer the proceeds directly to the poor.

But two empirical facts challenge this logic. First, long-run rental supply is far from perfectly elastic \citep{orr-1968,cy-1994,susin-2002,fack-2006,sy-2020,rs-2023}. Second, redistribution through the tax-and-transfer system is often incomplete or inefficient. In practice, governments rarely tax landlord profits fully or transfer those revenues directly to those most in need.

When supply is inelastic and redistribution fails, rent control may serve as a less inefficient tool for transferring surplus from wealthy landlords to poorer renters \citep{gyourko1989,arnott-1995}. This motivates our central normative question: can rent control improve welfare when market distortions coexist with imperfect fiscal tools?

We address this question by developing a quantitative general equilibrium model of rent control that incorporates heterogeneous households, realistic rental supply elasticities, and varying degrees of efficiency in redistributing landlord profits. The model captures key concerns raised by economists — including supply disincentives, misallocation, labor productivity distortions, eviction risk, and physical deterioration of the housing stock — alongside the redistribution and homelessness considerations emphasized by proponents of rent control. We calibrate the model to data from Toronto — a densely-populated, persistently unaffordable city — where tenancy control, one of the two most widely used rent control regimes, is in place alongside exemptions for buildings constructed in more recent years.\footnote{The Ontario Tenant Protection Act allowed landlords of controlled units to raise rents beyond the guideline amount upon tenant turnover, but required adherence to the guidelines otherwise. The Rent Control Act fully exempted units built after November 1, 1991.} 

We use the model to compare the long-run equilibrium under tenancy control with the long-run equilibria under full decontrol and the one under unit control — the second major rent control regime. Under tenancy control, landlords can raise rents freely upon tenant turnover, allowing rents for new tenants to clear the market. As a result, new tenants pay an entry premium relative to free-market rents — a pattern we confirm using 2016 Toronto data — and receive tenure-dependent rent discounts that accumulate only gradually over time. In contrast, unit control imposes rent caps regardless of tenant turnover that provide immediate discounts to new tenants but create shortages. As a result, allocation occurs through non-price mechanisms such as waiting lists or lotteries.

To align the model with empirical landlordship patterns, landlord profits accrue to the top 35\% of the wealth distribution. These profits are added to taxable income, which is taxed progressively, and any remaining excess revenue is redistributed via income tax reductions to the full population. We also explore scenarios in which revenues are rebated in a lump-sum fashion to renters and the homeless only.

Our first finding is that fully decontrolling the rental market would raise homelessness by 4.28\% but also raise welfare — measured in consumption-equivalent units using ex-ante expected utility of newborns — by 0.77\% relative to our benchmark with tenancy control. While tenancy control does partially shift resources toward poorer renters, it does so inefficiently, introducing sizable distortions that outweigh the gains from redistribution.

However, our model suggests that even though a unit control regime delivering the same average discount as the benchmark would increase the misallocation of controlled units, it would also lower homelessness by 16.93\% and raise welfare by 0.98\% relative to the benchmark. It would also outperform the full decontrol scenario in which excess government revenues are rebated through income tax cuts. While unit control involves luck in securing a controlled unit, it provides large upfront rent discounts to successful applicants. For newborns, this lottery-based discount would be more attractive than the gradual rent savings that accrue slowly over time in the benchmark with tenancy control.

In contrast, we demonstrate that if government revenues are rebated exclusively to renters and the homeless — rather than through income tax reductions — full decontrol would outperform unit control. 

Finally, we show that the welfare gains from unit control are not driven by redistribution, but by a binding minimum rental size constraint — highlighted by \citet{rs-2023} as a key reason many renters spend over 50\% of their income on rent. By reducing landlord maintenance, rent control lowers both housing expenditures and housing services consumption, bringing them closer to renters’ optimal bundles. A rent control regime narrowly targeted at curbing over-consumption yields an additional 1.02\% welfare gain relative to full decontrol and 1.79\% relative to the benchmark, while reducing homelessness by 42.88\% compared to the benchmark.

%In particular, rent control in our model improves welfare when used solely to curb housing services over-consumption, rather than to redistribute. As shown by \citet{rs-2023}, binding constraints on unit sizes play a key role in explaining why many renters spend over 50\% of their income on rent. These size constraints — arising from natural limitations, regulatory requirements, or safety standards — compel some renters to consume more housing services than they would in a frictionless market. In this context, the reduced maintenance associated with rent control reduces nominal rents and may bring housing consumption closer to renters’ optimal bundles. Although moving costs may cause tenants to remain even after services deteriorated below their optimal amount, our model suggests that the gains from the first effect would dominate. This channel, together with the general-equilibrium effects that reduce equilibrium rents substantially, would deliver an additional 1.02\% welfare gain relative to full decontrol and a 1.79\% gain relative to the benchmark, along with a 42.88\% reduction in homelessness compared to the benchmark.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:related_lit}

Economists have long argued that rent control distorts housing markets by capping rents below market-clearing levels, suppressing supply, creating excess demand, and misallocating units \citep{suen-1989,gl-2003,diamond-2019}. Classic textbook theory — developed primarily for unit control regimes — predicts that landlords will lower rental supply below the efficient level when rent ceilings bind, leading to persistent shortages and deadweight losses. Based on such positive analyses, economists have typically recommended removing rent control altogether \citep{lindbeck1971,buchanan_1986,friedman_stigler_1946}. Two key assumptions underpin this normative conclusion: that rental supply is infinitely elastic in the long run — so landlords earn no economic profits — and that should rental supply be inelastic, any profits that do arise can be costlessly and efficiently redistributed through lump-sum transfers.

However, a growing empirical and structural literature shows that rental supply is far from perfectly elastic, even in the long run \citep{orr-1968,cy-1994,susin-2002,fack-2006,sy-2020,rs-2023}. This implies that landlords often earn substantial profits that, in principle, could be taxed and redistributed. Yet in practice, governments face real constraints in fully taxing these profits and targeting the proceeds effectively to those in need. When these fiscal limitations are combined with realistic supply inelasticity, the standard recommendation to abolish rent control and rely solely on transfers becomes normatively ambiguous.

Recent work emphasizes that rent control’s effects on rental supply depend critically on policy design. For example, \citet{diamond-2019} and \citet{dmq-2019} exploit quasi-experimental variation from San Francisco’s expansion of tenancy control to estimate how supply, rents, and property values changed. They find that limiting rent increases for sitting tenants reduced rental supply and raised market rents for new tenants, harming newcomers on net. However, these studies do not conduct a welfare analysis across regimes. Our model formalizes this type of rental supply dynamic and shows that, under generous exemptions for recently constructed buildings, even large rent discounts in the controlled sector can preserve efficient supply. In contrast, when exemptions are limited or rent discounts are too deep, rent control reduces rental supply below its efficient level.

Several empirical studies examine how tenancy control clears the market. \citet{nagy-1997}, \citet{early-2000}, and \citet{cjq-2023} find that new tenants in regulated units often pay a premium relative to comparable free-market units. We confirm this pattern in our Toronto data, implying that under tenancy control, markets clear through prices paid by new tenants rather than through rationing. While \citet{arnott-1995} makes this point theoretically, our model formalizes this mechanism by endogenizing the entry premium: new tenants pay more upfront but receive tenure-dependent discounts.

From a theoretical perspective, \citet{fallis-1985} offered an early static partial equilibrium analysis of Toronto’s mixed rental market, emphasizing the importance of partial exemptions for newer buildings. \citet{be-2000} developed a static model of tenancy control with asymmetric information, showing how landlords set entry rents without knowing a tenant’s expected stay. We extend these insights by embedding tenancy control in a dynamic general equilibrium model with household income risk, endogenous labor productivity (which depends on the size of the rent discount), landlord neglect, and realistic rental supply elasticities — all calibrated to Toronto data.

General equilibrium models of rent control are rare. \citet{eric-1995} and \citet{fmn-2023} analyze unit control in dynamic life-cycle frameworks, but do not model tenancy control or endogenous eviction. While these studies evaluate the welfare of all households — including incumbent renters who already benefit from rent control — we instead focus on the ex-ante expected utility of newborns, offering a clean welfare criterion that is unaffected by incumbency status. This allows us to ask a normative question that, to our knowledge, has not been addressed in the macro-housing literature: can rent control enhance welfare when the government’s ability to redistribute landlord profits is limited?

We find that using rent control primarily as a redistributive tool reduces welfare, consistent with standard concerns. However, when used to curb over-consumption of housing services, rent control can enhance welfare substantially. This result builds on the findings in \citet{rs-2023}, who show that minimum unit size regulations induce low-income renters to consume more housing than they would in a frictionless market. In our model, rent control reduces landlord maintenance, which lowers housing services provided to controlled tenants. This decline, however, brings renters’ housing expenditures and their consumption of housing services closer to their optimal bundle. Demonstrating this, we highlight two distinct functions rent control can have: first, curbing housing over-consumption; and second, redistribution.

Beyond rents and allocation, rent control may also have significant implications for a major social issue: homelessness. Early analyses by \citet{tucker1987,tucker1989heritage,tucker1991cato,tucker1991public} estimated that rent control increased homelessness. However, a later study by \citet{eo-1998}, which to the best of our knowledge remains the most recent attempt to causally estimate the effect of rent control on homelessness using reliable micro-data, found that rent control either had no effect on homelessness or reduced it. Recent macro-housing models \citep{az-2024,abramson-2024} examine various housing policies' impact on homelessness, but do not analyze rent control directly. We tie these threads by showing how minimum rental sizes, rent control, and landlord neglect jointly shape renters’ housing consumption and rates of homelessness. Unlike the aforementioned studies, ours quantifies how homelessness responds to various rent control policies, and our prediction that rent control reduces homelessness is consistent with the findings of \citet{eo-1998}. We also show that the type of rent control and its specific design substantially matter for homelessness outcomes.

Finally, empirical evidence from decontrol episodes aligns with our model’s predictions. \citet{sims-2007} and \citet{app-2014} show that repealing rent control in Cambridge, Massachusetts raised house prices and average rents. These works provide a certain degree of external validation for our model simulations, which reproduce these patterns within our structural model of Toronto’s housing market.

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{General overview}\label{sec:brief_overview}

The model features overlapping generations of finitely lived households, along with price-taking, profit-maximizing rental and housing construction firms, and federal and local government entities. Households are heterogeneous with respect to age, labor market productivity, housing tenure (ownership, renting, and homelessness)\footnote{Beyond our substantive interest in homelessness, its inclusion in our model is essential. Without it, some low-income households would appear so consumption-constrained after paying for housing that even small rent reductions would generate unrealistically large welfare gains, driven by extremely high marginal utility of consumption of the very poor. Imposing a minimum income to ensure all households can afford at least the smallest rental is, in our view, a less satisfactory alternative, as the arbitrary choice of that threshold would itself distort welfare comparisons and bias results.}, housing assets, mortgage debt, and financial assets. Renters are further differentiated by the type of rental market in which they participate — either controlled or free — as well as by the duration of their residence in their unit. Working-age households participate in the labor market, while retirees receive government pensions that depend on their average lifetime earnings.

In the free market, rents are not a function of tenancy duration and landlords offset the depreciation of these units, thus maintaining constant housing services throughout a tenancy. In the controlled market, rents decline the longer a tenant remains in place, which encourages controlled renters to stay longer in their units than they would have if they were living in free-market units. However, the larger the discount they receive, the less likely they are to transition to or stay at higher labor productivities. Furthermore, the more their landlords can charge from a new tenant, the more likely they are to be evicted. Lastly, landlords of controlled rentals allow them to deteriorate by not offsetting their depreciation.\footnote{Deterioration in our model reflects empirical estimates of declines in physical attributes of the unit — such as aging paint, outdated flooring, worn walls, or older appliances and fixtures — rather than reductions in basic livability, like inadequate heating, pest infestations, or structural hazards, which fall under broader safety and human rights concerns and can be cause for lawsuits.}

Under tenancy control, renters choose between the controlled and free rental markets and decide on their tenancy duration. Under unit control, households enter a lottery for the chance to rent a controlled unit and receive an immediate discount relative to free-market rents. If they win the lottery and capitalize on it, they can remain in that unit as long as they wish, but their rents — adjusted for housing services — do not decline over time. Homeowners pay proportional property taxes and maintenance costs and finance their purchases through long-term mortgages contracts, following the approach of \citet{kkk-2021}.\footnote{Our general equilibrium results depend on how easily households can switch between renting and owning, as this affects rental demand and, consequently, equilibrium rents. Because mortgage contract features critically affect tenure decisions, we model mortgages as realistic long-term contracts.} 

A competitive, profit-maximizing rental firm supplies rental housing to both the free-market and rent-controlled segments, facing convex marginal management costs, depreciation, and property taxes. Housing stock built after a policy cut-off year is exempt from rent control if rented out, but can only be converted to rentals up to a certain maximum utilization rate — meaning that not all exempt stock can be turned into rental housing. The firm rents out as much exempt stock as possible — as long as this quantity remains below what the quantity supplied would be under full deregulation — and then uses the older stock to supply controlled units to meet any remaining rental demand, provided that the regulated rent covers the marginal cost of a controlled rental. If the controlled rent is too low, the firm supplies fewer older units, causing total rental supply to fall below the efficient competitive level despite the partial exemptions.

Under tenancy control, landlords can reset rents upon tenant turnover. Therefore, the initial controlled rent paid by new tenants clears the market for rent-controlled units by equating the aggregate demand for controlled rentals to the supply of controlled rentals. In contrast, under unit control, rents are below free-market rents regardless of tenant turnover. As a result, shortages arise, and access to controlled units is allocated randomly. In this case, the market-clearing object is the probability of securing a controlled unit via lottery.

Housing is supplied endogenously by a competitive, profit-maximizing construction company, which is subject to increasing marginal costs. The higher the housing price, the more it invests in the production of new housing, which supplements the depreciated housing stock. In equilibrium, the housing price equates the aggregate demand for housing (from both homeowners and renters) to the aggregate supply of housing.

The model incorporates both local and federal governments. The local government offers local public services to residents and homeless shelters to the homeless, financed through property taxes. The federal government funds pensions, lump-sum transfers, and federal public goods through income and consumption taxes.

Because the model economy represents a single city, we take the interest rate, $r$, as exogenous. 

\subsection{Demographics}\label{subsection:demographics}

Households enter the model at age $j=1$, retire from the labor force at age $J_{R}$, and live for $J$ periods. They face no mortality risk prior to age $J$, but die with certainty afterward. Household preferences are characterized by the following flow utility function:

\begin{equation*}
    u_{j}(c,h(o,f,\iota))=\xi_{j}^{\sigma}\frac{\left(c^{1-\gamma} h(o,f,\iota)^{\gamma}\right)^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma},
\end{equation*}

\noindent where $c$ denotes non-housing consumption and $h(o,f,\iota)$ represents housing services consumption, discussed further in Section \ref{subsection:housing_choice}. The consumption of housing services depends on the household's tenure status, $o$, rental market type, $f$, and tenancy duration, $\iota$. Specifically, $o=1$ indicates homeownership, while $o=0$ denotes renting. $f=1$ corresponds to renting in the free market and $f=0$ to renting in the controlled market.\footnote{For homeowners, the rental market indicator $f$ holds no significance, and we therefore set it to $1$.} The parameter $\iota$ represents the number of years a renter in the controlled market has resided in the same unit. The parameter $\gamma$ denotes the weight assigned to the consumption of housing services in the utility, $\sigma$ captures relative risk aversion and its inverse is the elasticity of intertemporal substitution, and $\xi_{j}$ is an age-specific equivalence scale that adjusts for changes in household size over their lifecycle, following \citet{lm-1980}, \citet{wz-2010}, and \citet{xu-2013}. Households discount future consumption at rate $\beta$ and pay a proportional tax $\tau_{c}$ on their non-housing consumption.

In addition, households derive warm-glow utility from bequests they leave behind upon death following \citet{cagetti-denardi-2006}. The bequest utility function is given by $\omega_{B}(q)=\mu \frac{q^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma}$, where $q$ denotes net worth at the end of life (formally defined in Section \ref{subsection:household_optimization}), and $\mu$ modulates the strength of the bequest motive.

\subsection{Income and income taxes}\label{subsection:income}

Working-age households ($j<J_{R}$) supply labor inelastically. Labor income for an age-$j$ household with labor productivity components $\chi$ and $e$ is given by $y_{\ell,j}(\chi,e)=\chi e\zeta_j$, where $\chi$ remains constant over the lifecycle and is drawn from the unconditional distribution $\hat{F}(\chi)$. The idiosyncratic shock $e$ evolves according to a Markov process $F(e,e')$. The term $\zeta_j$ captures the deterministic component of lifecycle earnings. Upon retirement, households receive pensions $y_{R}(\chi,e)$ based on their labor productivity at retirement.\footnote{Households with higher productivity at retirement are likely to have had higher earnings over their lives, and therefore receive larger pensions.}

In addition to labor income, households earn capital income on their savings: $y_{k}(a)=ar$, where $r$ is the interest rate. Taxable income is defined as:
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:taxable_labor_income}
  y^{x}_{j}(\chi,e,a) =y_{\ell,j}(\chi,e)+\nu y_{k}(a),
\end{equation*}

\noindent where $\nu$ denotes the taxable share of capital income. Taxable income is subject to a progressive tax schedule:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:income_tax}
  y^{\tau}_{j}(\chi,e,a) = y^{x}_{j}(\chi,e,a)-\tau_{\ell}y^{x}_{j}(\chi,e,a)^{\psi},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\tau_{\ell}$ determines the average tax rate and $\psi$ governs the progressivity of the tax schedule, following \citep{hsv-2020}.

As in \citet{kkk-2021}, living households across all ages receive financial endowments each period, which are equal to the bequests from the deceased in the preceding period. These are denoted by $\vartheta (\chi,e)$, capturing a positive correlation between bequests and household income.

Households receive lump-sum transfers $T(s)$, which may vary by household state $s$. These transfers depend on the profits earned by rental and construction firms, as discussed below. They are added to taxable income and then taxed as other income. As in \citet{kkk-2021}, to easily compare different equilibria, only the change in profits relative to the benchmark's profits is redistributed and taxed.

Lastly, households may also receive lump-sum transfers $T_f(s)$ from the federal government, which may also vary with the household’s state $s$.

\subsection{Housing}\label{subsection:housing_choice}

Households choose between owning or renting a dwelling, denoted by $o \in \mathcal{O}=\{0,1\}$, and the size of their dwelling, denoted by $h>0$. Households who cannot own or rent or decide not to, become homeless. We expand on the homelessness component in Section \ref{sec:cohabit_homeless}.

Household who purchase a house of size $h$ pay $p h$, with the restriction that homeowners must purchase a house no smaller than $\underline{h}_{o}$. Buying and selling houses involve proportional transaction costs, $\tau_{b} p h$ and $\tau_{s} p h$, respectively. All housing is subject to a property tax rate $\tau_{p}$ and depreciates at a rate $\delta$ per period. 

Renters must consume at least $0<\underline{h}_{r}<\underline{h}_{0}$ at the time of move-in \citep{gervais,sommeretal-2013,sommersullivan-2017,kaplanetal-2019,rs-2023}. Following \citet{rs-2023}, we calibrate the minimum rental size $\underline{h}_{r}$ to match the share of renters spending more than 50\% of their pre-tax income on housing. This parameter captures the extent to which some renters prefer to consume less housing services than $\underline{h}_{r}$ but are prevented from doing so by exogenous constraints — such as minimum size regulations imposed by municipalities.\footnote{See Section 9.5.4 of the Ontario Building Code: \url{https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/120332}.} To capture the idea that renters may choose to remain in their current unit — even without rent discounts — when their circumstances change, all renters face a fixed cost of moving between rentals, denoted by $\tau_r$.

\subsubsection{Unregulated rental market}

Free-market rents, denoted as $p_{r,1}$, are not a function of tenancy duration, and landlords fully offset the depreciation of their unit each period, maintaining housing services constant across a tenancy.

\subsubsection{Tenancy controlled rental market}

Under tenancy control, renters choose between the controlled and free rental markets, indicated by $f \in \{0,1\}$, where again $f=0$ denotes the controlled market and $f=1$ the free market. Rents in the controlled market decline over time as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rent_in_controlled_market}
\frac{\tilde{p}_{r,0}}{(1+\kappa_{1})^{\iota}} h.
\end{equation} 

\noindent $h$ is the size of the controlled unit at the time a new tenant moves in, $\tilde{p}_{r,0}$ is the initial rent paid by a new tenant, $\kappa_{1}$ is the rate of rent decline for each additional period the tenant remain in the same unit, and $\iota$ is the number of years the tenant has lived in the unit.

Landlords in the controlled market allow their units to deteriorate over time. As a result, continuing tenants in controlled units receive reduced housing services \citep{gl-1990,murray_et_al-1991}, given as follows:
\begin{equation}
h(o,f,\iota)=\frac{1}{(1+(1-o)(1-f)\kappa_{2})^{\iota}} h,
\end{equation}
 
\noindent where $\kappa_{2}$ is the rate of service decline per period for continuing tenants under tenancy control.\footnote{We do not track depreciated units. Once vacated, controlled units can be subdivided or restructured for new tenants.}

Figure \ref{fig:diagram_rent_path_skeleton} qualitatively illustrates controlled and free-market rents under tenancy control.\footnote{To maintain clarity in our illustration, we abstract from changes in service decline.} The downward-sloping blue line shows that rents in the controlled market begin at $\tilde{p}_{r,0}$ and decline with tenancy duration, as per equation \eqref{eq:rent_in_controlled_market}. The horizontal red line represents the free-market rent $p_{r,1}$, which is constant irrespective of tenancy duration. 

When both markets coexist, the free-market rent must exceed the average controlled rent; otherwise, there would be no incentive to supply free-market rentals. In contrast, in equilibrium, the free-market rent $p_{r,1}$, must be lower than the initial rent in the controlled market $\tilde{p}_{r,0}$. What explains this equilibrium outcome?

If initial rents in the controlled market were lower than free-market rents, $p_{r,1}$, all renters would choose the controlled market. To achieve equilibrium, therefore, the controlled market must feature an entry premium. Renters who expect to remain a short time in the unit would choose the free market to avoid this premium. In contrast, renters who expect to stay longer in the unit would pay the premium in exchange for longer term discounts. Because landlords cannot observe a renter's expected duration when they move in, they must set a uniform entry rent, and the market clears via self-selection.\footnote{While tenancy control could, in principle, induce landlords to discriminate among prospective tenants when setting entry rents, we find no significant impact of observable characteristics — such as age or income — on rents paid by new tenants (see Section \ref{section:appendix_discrimination} in the Appendix). This finding supports the view that large landlords — who dominate the supply of controlled units — rely on standardized pricing, lack information about expected tenancy duration of specific prospective tenants, and face legal constraints that limit this type of price discrimination. See \citet{be-2000} for further discussion.}

The premium paid in the controlled market ensures that shortages do not arise. While the point that shortages do not arise under tenancy control has been highlighted before in \citet{arnott-1995}, our framework shows that it also means that new movers in the controlled sector must pay a premium above free-market rents. This premium also allocates controlled units to those with the highest willingness to pay at the time they move into the unit. Although the initial (ex-ante) allocation of controlled units is relatively efficient, households' state variables change over time, which can reduce the efficiency of the initial allocation ex-post if controlled renters ``overstay their welcome''. 

Using 2016 Census data for Toronto, we estimate that new tenants in the controlled market pay a 5.9\% premium over free-market rents.\footnote{See Appendix Section \ref{section:appendix_measuring_premium}.} This result aligns with prior empirical studies \citep{nagy-1997,early-2000,cjq-2023}. Moreover, using this premium in our calibrated model with tenancy control, the allocation of controlled units is consistent with Toronto Census 2016 data.

\subsubsection{Unit control rental market}

Rents under unit control, denoted as $\hat{p}_{r,0}$, are regulated at the unit level and landlords cannot increase them upon tenant turnover. Thus, new tenants receive immediate discounts relative to free-market rents, meaning that $\hat{p}_{r,0}<p_{r,1}$. 

If controlled rents declined each period at a rate larger than the landlords' lack of maintenance, they would fall to zero in real terms in the long run. Therefore, in our model rents decline at the same rate as the reduction in services ($\kappa_1=\kappa_2$). Thus, while the nominal rent payment for a particular rental declines over time, the rent paid per quantity of housing services remains constant throughout a tenancy. 

Because there is no price mechanism to allocate controlled units under unit control, shortages arise, and thus a random draw $\eta$ determines which households gain access to controlled units. With probability $\pi$, a renter draws a favorable shock ($\eta=1$) and can rent a controlled unit, staying as long as they wish. Upon leaving their unit, they must draw a new favorable shock in order to re-enter the controlled market. With probability $1-\pi$, the draw is unfavorable ($\eta=0$), and the renter is excluded from the controlled market for that period. 

This highlights the tradeoff between unit control and tenancy control. Under tenancy control, prices allocate controlled units, resulting in a more efficient allocation than under unit control. However, tenants must forgo current consumption to enjoy the longer-term affordability benefits of rent control. In contrast, unit control provides an immediate discount relative to free-market rents to those who secure a controlled unit, but may exclude certain households with very high willingness to pay. 

Our simulations in Section \ref{sec:non_targeted_statistics} confirm that unit control results in fewer controlled units being allocated to low-income households, but it also shows that the allocation of controlled rentals under unit control is far from random. While the opportunity to rent a controlled unit — determined by lotteries — is random, the decision to rent a controlled unit and remain in it longer is not. Since low-income households are more likely to stay longer in the same unit, they ultimately receive a disproportionately larger share of controlled units in equilibrium.

\subsubsection{Impact of rent control on evictions}

A common argument against tenancy control is that it motivates landlords to evict long-term tenants in order to secure higher rents from new tenants. This incentive does not arise in free-market or unit-controlled settings, where landlords cannot charge higher rents to new tenants. To capture this mechanism, our model specifies an eviction probability that depends solely on the rent gap between what the landlord could charge a new tenant and what the current tenant pays.

Formally, renters face no eviction risk in free-market or unit-controlled rentals beyond ordinary turnover — which is not modeled explicitly here. Under tenancy control, however, the probability of eviction rises with the rent increase a landlord can gain by replacing the incumbent tenant with a new tenant. We specify the eviction probability $\epsilon$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eviction}
\epsilon(\iota)=
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if not a renter or renting a free-market or unit-controlled rental} \\ \epsilon_1 \frac{1}{\left(1 + \kappa_1 - \kappa_2 \right)^{\iota}} & \text{if in a tenancy-controlled rental}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent Here, $\epsilon_1$ is a scale parameter that determines how strongly the rent gap influences eviction risk. We calibrate $\epsilon_1$ to match the observed rate of evictions due to reasons other than non-payment of rent, which we interpret as landlord-driven displacement motivated by rent increases.

Renters who are evicted from tenancy-controlled or unit-controlled markets re-enter the next period as free-market renters, with their housing market state reset to $f=1$.

\subsubsection{Impact of rent control on labor productivity}

To reflect the widely documented distortions that rent control creates for labor mobility, our model specifies the Markov process for stochastic labor productivity as an endogenous function of households’ choices to reside in a controlled unit and the depth of their rent discount.

Specifically, controlled renters with larger discounts are less likely to move to higher-income states or to remain in a given high-income state. We define the discount $\aleph$ as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:discount}
\aleph(\iota)=\begin{cases}
1-\frac{1}{\left(1+\kappa_1-\kappa_2 \right)^{\iota}} & \text{under tenancy control} \\[8pt]
1-\frac{\hat{p}_{r,0}}{p_{r,1}} & \text{under unit control}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

We further define the penalty $\Upsilon$ using the sigmoid function as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:penalty}
\Upsilon(\iota,\chi,e,j)=1-\aleph(\iota) \times  \kappa_3\,\frac{1}{1+e^{-\kappa_4\big( y_{\ell,j}(\chi,e)-\hat{y}\big)}},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\hat{y}$ denotes the median income of renters, $\kappa_3$ determines the strength of the penalty, and $\kappa_4$ controls how strongly the penalty affects high-income renters relative to low-income renters. Higher values of $\kappa_4$ reduce the penalty for low-income renters and increase it for high-income renters.

Lastly, the Markov chain for $e$'s is adjusted so that for $e$'s below the maximum productivity level and above the smallest productivity, $F(e,e'\mid\iota,\chi,e,j,e'\geq e)=(1-\Upsilon(\iota,\chi,e,j))\,F(e,e'\mid e'\geq e)$, for the $e$ that is equal to the smallest productivity, $F(e,e'\mid\iota,\chi,e,j,e'>e)=(1-\Upsilon(\iota,\chi,e,j))\,F(e,e')$, and for the $e$ that is equal to the maximum productivity, $F(e,e\mid\iota,\chi,e,j)=(1-\Upsilon(\iota,\chi,e,j))\,F(e,e)$. All remaining probabilities adjust accordingly to ensure that $\sum_{e^{'}} F(e,e')=1$ for every $e$.

\subsubsection{Homelessness}\label{sec:cohabit_homeless}

Because rental housing has a binding minimum size, some households may not want or be able to participate in the conventional rental housing market. Those households face homelessness $\left(b \in \mathcal{O}\right)$. As in \citet{az-2024}, they receive reduced housing services free-of-charge $\underline{h}_{h}$ while homeless, where $0<\underline{h}_{h}<\underline{h}_{r}$. However, as in \citet{abramson-2024}, the city incurs a cost $\theta_{b}$ per homeless household. 

For households who are homeless, we set $o=0$ and $f=1$ when we define the dynamic program later.

\subsection{Mortgages}\label{subsection:morgtages}

In addition to rent control, the structure of mortgage contracts also influences households' tenure decisions, which in turn affect rental demand, equilibrium rents, and welfare results. Modeling mortgages as realistic long-term contracts is therefore important. We model mortgages, denoted as $m$, as long-term contracts with origination costs, prepayment penalties, and loan-to-value (LTV) and payment-to-income (PTI) constraints at origination only. We set the mortgage interest rate at $r_{m}=r+\Delta_m$, where $r$ is the return on financial assets, and $\Delta_m$ is the mortgage debt interest rate premium \citep{sommeretal-2013,sommersullivan-2017,kaplanetal-2019}. Consistent with Canadian tax policy, mortgage interest payments cannot be deducted from taxable income.

To obtain a new mortgage, households must pay a fixed cost $\omega_{1}$. A mortgage can only be originated if the LTV ratio does not exceed a threshold $\lambda_{m,1}(ph)<1$, determined by the house value at the time of purchase $ph$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LTV}
\frac{m}{p h} \leq \lambda_{m,1}(ph).
\end{equation}

\noindent The PTI constraint caps mortgage debt based on the borrower's pre-tax income at the time of origination: 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PTI}
\frac{\tau_{p}ph+(r_{m}+\upsilon_{1})m}{y_{\ell,j}(\chi,e)+y_{k}(a)} \leq \lambda_{m,2}.
\end{equation}

\noindent $\upsilon_{1}$ represents the average annual fraction repaid over the mortgage term, and $\lambda_{m,2}$ is the maximum PTI ratio permitted. This constraint ensures that the sum of property taxes, interest payments, and average principal repayments does not exceed a fixed share $\lambda_{m,2}$ of the borrower's pre-tax labor and capital income.

Each period, mortgage holders must allocate at least a fraction $\upsilon_{1}$ of the outstanding balance for repayment, and may pay up to a fraction $\upsilon_{2}>\upsilon_{1}$ of the debt without penalty. Payment above this threshold incurs a prepayment penalty:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prepayment_penalty}
\tau_{pp}(m,m')=\omega_{2}\mbox{max}\left\{(1-\upsilon_{2})m-m',0\right\}.
\end{equation}

\noindent We consider borrowers paying less than $\upsilon_{1}m$, as ones who must originate a new mortgage. Therefore, they must incur the fixed cost $\omega_{1}$ and adhere to constraints \eqref{eq:LTV}--\eqref{eq:PTI} once again.

\subsection{Summary of heterogeneity}

Households are heterogeneous along several dimensions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Financial assets, $a\in \mathcal{A}=\mathbb{R}_{+}$.
    \item Housing tenure status, $o \in \mathcal{O}=\{0,1\}$.
    \item Opportunity to rent in the controlled market in the case of unit control $\eta \in \mathcal{O}$.
    \item Rental market type for renters, $f \in \mathcal{O}$.
    \item Duration of residence in the same unit for rent-controlled tenants, $\iota \in \mathcal{I}=\mathbb{N}$.
    \item Housing size, $h\in \mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}_{++}$.
    \item Mortgage debt, $m \in \mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}_{+}$.
    \item Idiosyncratic labor fixed effect $\chi \in \mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}_{++}$.
    \item Stochastic labor productivity shock, $e \in \mathcal{E} = \mathbb{R}_{++}$.
\end{itemize}
 
\noindent We denote the measure of age-$j$ households over endowments by $\Psi_{j}(a,o,\eta,f,\iota,h,m,\chi,e)$. For brevity, to represent the state of a household whose age is $j$, we mainly employ the notation $s$.

\subsection{Dynamic program}\label{subsection:household_optimization}
Given its state variables, a household must make several decisions: saving ($a'$), housing tenure ($o'$), whether to be homeless ($b'$), and housing consumption ($h'$). Under tenancy control, renters also decide whether to rent in the controlled or free market ($f'_{c}$, where we set $f'_{c}=1$ whenever $o'=1$ or $b'=1$). We use $f'_c$ to denote the household's intended choice of rental market, distinguishing it from the realized $f'$. This is important because, in the event of eviction, $f'$ is forced to equal 1 regardless of the value of $f'_c$.
Renters in the controlled market also choose whether to extend their tenancy by one period ($\iota'_c$). For the decision of whether to remain in the same unit, we similarly define $\iota'_c$ as the household's intended duration choice, which may differ from the realized $\iota'$. If an eviction occurs, the household is forced to start anew, so $\iota'$ is set to 1 even if they had planned to stay longer (i.e., $\iota'_c > 1$). Homeowners choose whether to originate a new mortgage ($n$) and their mortgage debt ($m'$). At the terminal age $J$, households also decide how much bequest to leave behind ($q'$).

To economize on space and avoid writing out multiple dynamic problems, we incorporate both tenancy control and unit control into a single budget constraint. We define the rent control regime by $\alpha \in \{0,1\}$, where $\alpha=0$ corresponds to tenancy control and $\alpha=1$ to unit control.

The household's dynamic optimization problem is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vf}
\begin{aligned}
V_{j}(s) 
= \max_{\substack{\{c,\, a',\, o',\, b',\, f'_c, \\ \iota'_c,\, h',\, n,\, m',\, q'\}}} \Big\{
& \; u_{j}\big( c,\, h'( o', f'_c, \iota'_c ) \big)
+ \mathbbm{1}_{\{ j = J \}}\, \omega_{B}(q')+ \\
& \mathbbm{1}_{\{ j < J \}}\, \beta 
\int_{\mathcal{E}} \Big[
(1 - \epsilon(\iota'_c))\, V_{j+1}\big( s'( f' = f'_c,\, \iota' = \iota'_c ) \big)dF(e, e' \mid f'_c, \iota'_c, \chi, e, b') +\\ & \quad \epsilon(\iota'_c)\, V_{j+1}\big( s'( f' = 1,\, \iota' = 1 ) \big)dF(e, e' \mid f'_c, \iota'_c, \chi, e, b') \,  \Big]
\Big\},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent subject to the following constraints:
\begin{flalign}
  & c(1+\tau_{c})+a'+or_{m}m+(1-o')\left[(1-f'_{c})\frac{\left((1-\alpha)\tilde{p}_{r,0}+\alpha \hat{p}_{r,0}\right)} {(1+\kappa_{1})^{\iota'_{c}-1}}h'(0)+(1-b')f'_{c}p_{r,1}h'\right]+ \label{eq:bc} \\ & \quad \mathbbm{1}_{\left\{o=0 \land o'=0 \land f'_c \neq f \land h'\neq h\right\}}\tau_r+o\left(\tau_{p}+\delta_{h}+\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{o'=0 \vee h'\neq h\right\}}\tau_{s}\right)ph + o'(1+\tau_{b}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{o=0 \vee h'\neq h\right\}})ph'+ \notag \\ & \quad \omega_{1}n+ (1-n)\tau_{pp}(m,m')+\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{o'=1 \land j=J\right\}}ph(\tau_{p}-\delta_{h}-\tau_{s})= y_{\ell,j}(\chi,e)+y_{k}(a)-y^{\tau}_{j}(\chi,e,a)+\notag \\ & \quad a+o(ph-m)+ o'm'+\vartheta(\chi,e)+(1-\overline{\tau}_{\ell})T(s)+T_f(s) \notag \\
    & a' \geq 0 \label{eq:financial_wealth} \\
    & h' \geq o'\underline{h}_{o}+(1-b')(1-o')\underline{h}_{r} \label{eq:min_house_size} \\
    & h'=b'\underline{h}_{h} \label{eq:min_house_size_homeless} \\
    & f'_{c} = 1 \quad \text{if $\eta=0$ and $\alpha=1$}\label{eq:free_market} \\
    & h'(o',f'_{c},\iota'_{c})=\frac{1}{(1+(1-o')(1-f'_{c})\kappa_{2})^{\iota'_{c}-1}}h' \label{eq:housing servies_controlled}\\
    & n \in \left\{0,o'\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{j<J_{R}\right\}}\right\} \label{eq:origination} \\
    & nm' \leq \lambda_{m,1}(ph') p h' \label{eq:LTV_1} \\
    & n (\tau_{p}ph'+(r_{m}+\upsilon_{1})m')\leq \lambda_{m,2}(y_{\ell,j}(\chi,e)+y_{k}(a))\label{eq:PTI_1} \\
    & (1-n)m'\leq (1-\upsilon_{1})m \label{eq:settle_debt} \\
    & q' = a' + o' \left[p h'(1-\tau_p-\delta-\tau_s)-(1+r_m)m'\right] \label{eq:networth}\\
    & \iota'_{c} = 1 \quad \text{if $f'_{c}=1$,}\label{eq:iota_restriction}
\end{flalign} 

\noindent The household's utility function comprises three components: a flow utility derived from housing and non-housing consumption, a warm-glow component stemming from bequests left behind by deceased households, and a discounted value of future utility which depends on whether the household was evicted or not.\footnote{The dynamic program is written to allow for labor productivity shocks across all ages, facilitating a unified representation of decision-making for both retirees and working-age individuals.}

Equation \eqref{eq:bc} defines the household's budget constraint. The left-hand side includes expenditures (in order of appearance): consumption and consumption taxes, savings, mortgage interest payments, rental costs in controlled and free markets, moving costs for renters switching units, property taxes, housing depreciation, and housing transaction costs (buying/selling), mortgage origination costs, prepayment penalties, and property taxes, depreciation, and selling costs for terminal homeowners. The right-hand side comprises resources available for expenditure (in order of appearance): after-tax labor and capital incomes, financial assets, housing equity, mortgage funds raised, bequests contingent upon the household's labor productivity, lump-sum transfers — net of tax — from the rental and construction companies that depend on the household's state, and lump-sum transfers, also net of tax, from the federal government that may also be conditional on the state of the household. In our calibration, we rebate profits exclusively to households in the top of the wealth distribution, consistent with empirical evidence on the concentration of landlords at the top of the wealth distribution.

The remaining constraints specify behavioral and institutional limits: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Constraint \eqref{eq:financial_wealth}: Households cannot borrow without collateral (no unsecured debt).
    \item Constraint \eqref{eq:min_house_size}: Housing consumption constraint for owners and renters. All owners must consume at least $\underline{h}_{o}$ units of housing, all renters must consume at least $\underline{h}_{r}$.
    \item Constraint \eqref{eq:min_house_size_homeless}: Housing consumption for households who are homeless. Households experiencing homelessness must consume exactly $\underline{h}_{h}$.
    \item Constraint \eqref{eq:free_market}: Under unit control, households cannot rent in the controlled market if they draw a bad shock ($\eta=0$).
    \item Constraint \eqref{eq:housing servies_controlled}: Renters in controlled units experience declining housing services over time due to landlord neglect. 
    \item Constraint \eqref{eq:origination}: Only working-age households may originate new mortgages.
    \item Constraints \eqref{eq:LTV_1} and \eqref{eq:PTI_1}: Loan-to-value and payment-to-income constraints at origination.
    \item Constraint \eqref{eq:settle_debt}: Mortgage holders must repay at least a minimum fraction $\upsilon_{1}$ of their principal each period.
    \item Constraint \eqref{eq:networth}: Bequests equal net financial and net housing wealth at death, accounting for the mortgage, property taxes, depreciation, and selling costs.
    \item Constraint \eqref{eq:iota_restriction}: Years in the same rental constraint. For households who do not rent in the controlled market, we mechanically set the number of years in the same rental $\iota'_{c}$, to 1.
\end{itemize} 

Finally, we denote policy functions for households as follows: consumption $c_{j}(s)$, savings $a'_{j}(s)$, homelessness $b'_{j}(s)$, housing tenure $o'_{j}(s)$, rental market choice $f'_{c,j}(s)$, tenure duration in rent-controlled housing $\iota'_{c,j}(s)$, housing size $h'_{j}(s)$, mortgage debt $m'_{j}(s)$, mortgage origination $n_{j}(s)$, and bequests $q'_{j}(s)$.

\subsection{Rental company with partial exemptions}\label{sec:rental_company}

A key concern with any rent control policy is its potential impact on rental housing supply. To address this, we develop a theoretical framework that extends and deepens the rental supply ideas in \citet{fallis-1985}. Moreover, to generate a realistically finite rental supply elasticity we exploit the convex management costs approach in \citet{garrigaeetal-2009}. We extend both frameworks to model partial rent control exemptions tied to the housing stock’s year of construction and its potential for rental utilization. Our framework captures how, even with such exemptions, rent control policies can result in sub-competitive rental supply levels.

The rental company is a price-taker and supplies housing in two segments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Free market segment} ($i=1$): Housing built after a policy cut-off year, denoted as $Y_{c}$, is legally exempt from rent control. However, not all of this exempt housing stock can be converted to rental use. Let $H'_1$ denote the stock built after year $Y_{c}$, and let $\phi \in (0,1)$ represent the maximum rental utilization rate of the unregulated stock. In practice, legal restrictions (such as condo by-laws and co-op rules that prohibit renting certain units) and heterogeneous owner preferences impose a binding upper limit on the fraction of this exempt stock that can be rented out. We capture this institutional and behavioral constraint by imposing an exogenous cap on the rental utilization rate for the exempt stock. To calibrate $\phi$, we use census data from Calgary and Edmonton — two large Canadian cities without rent control — to measure the average rental utilization rates for condominiums and single-family homes. We then weight these averages by Toronto’s actual mix of condos and single-family homes to obtain an estimated rental utilization rate of any unregulated stock of 46.8\%.
    \item \textbf{Controlled segment} ($i=0$): Older housing defined as $H_0'=H'-H_1'$, are subject to rent control if rented out, which can only earn the expected controlled rent of $\overline{p}_{r,0}$.
\end{itemize}

To supply $X'=X'_0+X'_1$ rentals in total, the firm incurs convex management costs: $C(X')=\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_2}X'^{\theta_2}$ with $\theta_2>1.$ \citep{garrigaeetal-2009}. Each unit also incurs depreciation $\delta$ and property taxes $\tau_p$, and housing can be bought at price $p$, which is its resale value net of depreciation and taxes.

Let $X'^{*}$ denote the unconstrained competitive rental quantity when the exempt housing stock $H'_1$ equals the total housing stock $H'$. This unconstrained amount satisfies: \[MC(X'^{*}) = p^{*}_{r,1},\] where $p^{*}_{r,1}$ is the competitive rent in the absence of rental market regulations. Therefore, as long as $\phi H'_1<X'^{*}$, it is optimal for the firm to first fully utilize the available exempt stock for the supply of free-market rentals and supply:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uncontrolled_optimization}
X_1'=\phi H'_1\leq X'^{*}.  
\end{equation}

The controlled supply is derived from the marginal cost curve of the firm, evaluated at the expected controlled rent $\overline{p}_{r,0}$, as follows: 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:controlled_optimization}
X_0'=\mbox{min}\{\text{max}\left\{X_0'^*,0\right\},X'^*-X'_1\}, \quad
\text{where}\quad MC(X_0'^*)=\overline{p}_{r,0}.    
\end{equation}

\noindent In the controlled sector, the firm will only produce up to the point where the marginal rental cost — at $X_0'$ — is equal to the controlled rent. However, if the controlled rent is sufficiently large, such that $X_0'^*>X'^*-X'_1$, then the firm will only supply the difference to reach the efficient quantity.

Conditional on the government’s choice of $H'_1$ and the expected controlled rent $\overline{p}_{r,0}$, which is either an outcome of government intervention or directly set by policy, the rental firm’s dynamic program is given by:
\begin{align}\label{eq:rental_optimization}
V(X) &= \max_{\{X_0',X_1'\}} \Big\{\overline{p}_{r,0}X_0'+p_{r,1} X_1'-\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_2}(X')^{\theta_2}-p X'+p(1-\delta-\tau_p)X+\frac{1}{1+r} V(X')\Big\},
\end{align}
\noindent subject to \eqref{eq:uncontrolled_optimization}-\eqref{eq:controlled_optimization} and:
\begin{align}
& X'=X_0'+X_1'. \label{eq:total_supply_def}
\end{align}

\noindent The marginal cost curve is therefore \[MC(X')=\theta_1(X')^{\theta_2-1}+p\left(\frac{r+\delta+\tau_p}{1+r}\right)\]

\subsection{Implications of rental company set up}

This rental supply framework highlights several policy-relevant implications:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Partial exemptions alone do not guarantee efficient supply.} 
Even if the government allows housing built after a given year $Y_{c}$ to be rented at free-market rents, the rental company will only supply up to their optimal condition \eqref{eq:uncontrolled_optimization}. If this produces a smaller quantity in the free market than the unconstrained competitive quantity $X'^*$, then the firm must rely on the controlled segment to close the gap. However, if the regulated rent $\overline{p}_{r,0}$ is too low to cover the marginal cost at the controlled supply $X'_0=X'^*-X'_1$, the firm supplies less of the older stock in the rental market, resulting in under-production: $X'=X'_1+X'_0<X'^*$.

\item \textbf{Expanding the exemption window weakly increases supply.} 
If the government lowers the exemption cut-off year $Y_{c}$ — for example, reducing it from 1991 to 1980 — then the stock of housing eligible for free-market rents $H'_1$, increases. This raises the free-market supply $X_1'$ and thus allows the firm to supply more rental housing at market rents without relying on the controlled segment. In turn, this reduces any potential gap between actual quantity supplied and the efficient quantity.

\item \textbf{More stringent rent control weakly reduces supply relative to the competitive quantity.} 
Lowering the controlled rent $\overline{p}_{r,0}$ reduces the incentive to supply older units if it falls below the marginal cost at the original supply of $X_0'$. In this case, it opens a potentially gap between the efficient competitive quantity and the actual quantity supplied. Moreover, since the optimal quantity of controlled units is given by \[X_0'=\min\left\{\text{max}\left\{\left[\frac{1}{\theta_{1}}\left(\overline{p}_{r,0}-p\left(\frac{r+\delta_{h}+\tau_{p}}{1+r}\right)\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{\theta_{2}-1}},0\right\},\, X'^*-X_1'\right\},\]
it follows that if $\overline{p}_{r,0}$ is lower than  
$p\left(\frac{r+\delta_{h}+\tau_{p}}{1+r} \right)$, then $X_0'=0$. Therefore, if rent control is too stringent, the controlled market collapses entirely.

\end{itemize}

Finally, we define the profits of the rental company each period as:

\begin{equation}
    \Pi_r=\overline{p}_{r,0}X_0'+p_{r,1} X_1'-\left[\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_2}(X')^{\theta_2}+p(\delta+\tau_p) X'\right]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Housing construction company}\label{sec:construction_company}

A competitive construction firm produces new housing \citep{rotberg-2022}. It earns revenue $pI$, from its production of new housing $I$. The firm's total cost of production is given by the technology:
\begin{equation*}
TC(I)=\frac{\varsigma_{1}}{\varsigma_{2}}I^{\varsigma_{2}}\label{eq:total_cost_structure},
\end{equation*}

\noindent where $\varsigma_{1}$ dictates the average cost of housing investment, and $\varsigma_{2}$ is the price elasticity of housing investment. The firm chooses $I$ to maximize profits:
\begin{equation*}
\label{eq:construction_company_profits}
\Pi_h= \underset{I} {\text{$\mbox{max}$}}{\left\{p I-TC(I)\right\}}.
\end{equation*}

\noindent The first-order condition for this problem implies the following standard relationship between housing investment and the price of housing:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:construction_company_first_order_condition}
I^{\star}=\varsigma_{1} p^{\varsigma_{2}-1}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Aggregation}\label{subsection:aggregation}

As mentioned in Section \ref{subsection:income}, we redistribute only the change in rental and construction company profits relative to the benchmark's profits, which we denote as $\Pi_b=\Pi_{r,b}+\Pi_{h,b}$. Thus, the change relative to benchmark profits must equal the total lump-sum transfers from rental and construction companies to households, leading to the following constraint:
\begin{align}\label{eq:rebates}
   (\Pi_h+\Pi_r)-\Pi_b=\sum_{j=1}^J \int_\mathcal{S} T(s)\mathrm{d}\Psi(s).
\end{align}

As outlined in Section \ref{sec:brief_overview}, our model incorporates both a local and a federal government. The local government collects property taxes, and provides local public goods to residents, which do not enter into households' utility, as well as homeless shelters to the homeless. Therefore, the budget constraint of the local government is as follows:
\begin{align}\label{eq:gbc_1}
  G_l +\sum_{j=1}^J \int_\mathcal{S} \theta_{b} b'_{j}(s)\mathrm{d}\Psi(s) = \tau_{p} p H.
\end{align}

\noindent In calibrating the model to match the existing tax code in Toronto, we set the level of consumption of local public goods, denoted as $G_l$, to ensure budget balance. In all policy experiments, we hold $G_l$ constant and adjust the property tax rate $\tau_{p}$ to satisfy the budget constraint.

The federal government collects income and consumption taxes and provides federal public goods, $G_{f}$, which also do not enter households' utility, pensions to retirees, and lump-sum transfers to households $T_{f}(s)$. As in the case of the local government, we set the level of $G_{f}$ to ensure budget balance, and in our experiments, we hold $G_{f}$ at its benchmark level and adjust either $\tau_\ell$, which determines the average labor income tax to balance the budget, or $T_{f}(s)$ to maintain the federal budget is balanced. Thus, we require that:
\begin{align}\label{eq:gbc_2}
   G_{f}+\sum_{j=R}^J \int_\mathcal{S} y_{R}(s)\mathrm{d}\Psi(s)+\sum_{j=1}^J \int_\mathcal{S} T_f(s) \mathrm{d}\Psi(s)=\sum_{j=1}^J \int_\mathcal{S} \left(y^{\tau}_{j}(s)+\tau_{c}c_{j}(s)\right)\mathrm{d}\Psi(s) 
\end{align}

Before presenting the remaining market-clearing conditions, we denote $S=A\times O \times O \times O \times I \times H\times M\times X \times E$ as a typical subset of the state space $\mathcal{S}$, and $A$, $O$, $I$, $H$, $M$, $X$, and $E$ as typical subsets of $\mathcal{A}$, $\mathcal{O}$, $\mathcal{I}$, $\mathcal{H}$, $\mathcal{M}$, $\mathcal{X}$, and $\mathcal{E}$, respectively.

We require that the aggregate housing demand equals aggregate housing supply:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:housing_clearing_condition}
  \sum_{j=1}^J \int_\mathcal{S}  \left(\frac{(1-b'_{j}(s))h'_{j}(s)}{\left[(1+(1-o'_{j}(s))(1-f'_{c,j}(s))\kappa_{2}\right]^{\iota'_{c,j}(s)-1}}\right) \mathrm{d}\Psi(s) = H',
\end{equation}

\noindent where the law of motion of the housing stock is: 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:housing_stock_lom}
H'=I+(1-\delta)H.
\end{equation}

\noindent Equation \eqref{eq:housing_stock_lom} is a standard law of motion for housing, stating that the next period's housing stock equals new investment plus the depreciated stock of housing. 

We also impose market-clearing conditions for rental housing. In each rental market, aggregate rental demand equals aggregate rental supply:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rental_housing_clearing_condition}
  \sum_{j=1}^J \int_\mathcal{S} (1-o'_{j}(s))\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{f'_{c,j}(s)=i\right\}}\left(\frac{(1-b'_{j}(s))h'_{j}(s)}{\left[1+(1-f'_{c,j}(s))\kappa_{2}\right]^{\iota'_{c,j}(s)}}\right) \mathrm{d}\Psi(s) = X'_{i} \quad i \in \{0,1\}.
\end{equation}

The distributions of households evolve according to the following laws of motion,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lom1}
\Psi_{1}(S) = \int_{\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{E}} \mathbbm{1}_{\big\{ (0,0,0,0,0,0,0,X,E) \in S \big\}} d\hat{F}(X)\, \tilde{F}(E), \quad \text{under tenancy control.}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lom1_2}
\begin{split}
\Psi_{1}(S) = & \, \eta \int_{\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{E}} \mathbbm{1}_{\big\{ (0,0,1,0,0,0,0,X,E) \in S \big\}} d\hat{F}(X)\, \tilde{F}(E)+ \\ & (1-\eta) \int_{\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{E}} \mathbbm{1}_{\big\{ (0,0,0,0,0,0,0,X,E) \in S \big\}} d\hat{F}(X)\, \tilde{F}(E), \quad \text{under unit control.}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lom2}
\begin{split}
\Psi_{j+1}(S) = \int_{\mathcal{S}} \biggl\{ 
& [1-\epsilon(\iota)]\, \hat{F}(X)\, F(E,e | f'_{c,j}(s), \iota'_{c,j}(s), \chi, e, b'_j(s))\, \mathbbm{1}_{\{ f' = f'_{c,j}(s) \}}\, L_j(s)+ \\ & \, \epsilon(\iota)\, \hat{F}(X)\, F(E,e | f'_{c,j}(s), \iota'_{c,j}(s), \chi, e, b'_j(s))\, \mathbbm{1}_{\{ f' = 1 \}} \times \, L_j(s)
\biggr\} d\Psi_j(s), \quad j=1,\ldots,J-1,
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent where
\[ \quad L_j(s) = \mathbbm{1}_{\big\{ a'_j(s) \in A,\, o'_j(s) \in O,\, b'_j(s) \in O,\, \iota'_{c,j}(s) \in I,\, h'_j(s) \in H,\, m'_j(s) \in M \big\}}.\]

Finally, total bequests from deceased households must equal total wealth endowments received by living households:
\begin{equation}\label{market_clearing:agg_bequests}
    \int_{\mathcal{S}} q'_{J}(s) \mathrm{d}\Psi_{J}(s) = \sum_{j=1}^J \int_{\mathcal{S}} \vartheta (\chi,e)\ \mathrm{d}\Psi_j(s).
\end{equation}

\noindent Following \citet{kkk-2021}, we choose the parameter $\vartheta$ to ensure that this condition holds in all our experiments. Consequently, variations in $\vartheta$ across experiments are contingent upon proportional changes in aggregate bequests.

\subsection{Equilibrium}
An equilibrium in the model consists of: 
\begin{itemize}
   \item A sequence of aggregate prices and     quantities:\\   
    $\left\{H', I, \alpha, \{X'_{i}\}_{i=0}^{1},    \overline{p}_{r,0}, p_{r,1}, \Pi_r, \Pi_h, \tilde{p}_{r,0}, \hat{p}_{r,0}, \pi, p, \tau_{\ell}, \overline{\tau}_{\ell}, \{T(s), T_f(s)\}_{s \in \mathcal{S}}, \vartheta \right\}$;
    \item A sequence of value and policy functions: \\
    $\biggl\{V_{j}(\cdot),c_{j}(\cdot),a'_{j}(\cdot),o'_{j}(\cdot),b'_{j}(\cdot),f'_{c,j}(\cdot),\iota'_{c,j}(\cdot),h'_{j}(\cdot),m'_{j}(\cdot),n_{j}(\cdot),q'_{j}(\cdot)\biggl\}_{j=1}^J$; and
    \item A sequence of measures: 
     $\left\{\Psi_{j}(\cdot)\right\}_{j=1}^J$,
\end{itemize}
\noindent such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The policy and value functions solve the household's dynamic problem \eqref{eq:vf} subject to constraints \eqref{eq:bc}--\eqref{eq:networth};
\item Rental supply in both markets $\{X'_{i}\}^{1}_{i=0}$, solves the rental company's problem as defined in \eqref{eq:rental_optimization};
\item Housing investment $I$, solves the construction company's first-order condition, \eqref{eq:construction_company_first_order_condition};
\item The lump-sum transfers from the rental and construction companies $\{T(s)\}_{s \in \mathcal{S}}$, satisfy constraint \eqref{eq:rebates};
\item The property tax $\tau_p$, balances local government's budget \eqref{eq:gbc_1};
\item Either $\tau_{\ell}$ or $\{T_{f}(s)\}_{s\in \mathcal{s}}$ balance the federal government's budget \eqref{eq:gbc_2};
\item The housing price $p$, clears the housing market
\eqref{eq:housing_clearing_condition}, subject to the housing stock law of motion \eqref{eq:housing_stock_lom};
\item The free-market rent $p_{r,1}$, clears the free rental market (\eqref{eq:rental_housing_clearing_condition}, when $i=1$); and
\begin{itemize}
    \item Under tenancy control, the initial controlled rent $\tilde{p}_{r,0}$ equates the aggregate demand for controlled rentals to the aggregate supply of controlled units (\eqref{eq:rental_housing_clearing_condition}, with $i=0$); or
    \item Under unit control, the probability $\pi$ that the random shock $\eta=1$, which determines whether a renter can secure a controlled unit, clears the controlled market under unit control (\eqref{eq:rental_housing_clearing_condition}, when $i=0$);
\end{itemize}
\item The laws of motion for the distribution of households, \eqref{eq:lom1}--\eqref{eq:lom2}, evolve in accordance with households' policy functions; and
\item $\vartheta$ equates aggregate bequests left by descendants to the aggregate bequests provided to all living households, \eqref{market_clearing:agg_bequests}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Calibration}\label{sec:calibration}

Since Toronto's rental market operates with tenancy control regulations, we calibrate our model with tenancy control ($\alpha=0$). Later we explore how the calibrated model changes when unit control is imposed instead.

The calibration of our model proceeds in two steps. First, we calibrate specific parameters using data from Toronto and existing estimates from the literature. Second, we calibrate the remaining parameters to match certain model-generated statistics with their corresponding moments in our Toronto data. Table \ref{tab:calibration_external} summarizes the externally assigned parameters and their sources. Table \ref{tab:calibration_internal} lists the internally calibrated parameters along with the corresponding empirical targets. In Section \ref{sec:non_targeted_statistics}, we assess the model's performance on non-targeted dimensions.

In Section \ref{appendix:data} of the online appendix, we provide details on how we constructed key statistics from various datasets. 

\subsection{Assigned parameter values}

\subsubsection{Demographics, preferences, and income}
A model period is one year. Households enter the model at age 26 and live up to age 85, and retire at age 66. This implies a retirement age of $J_{R}=41$ and a total lifespan of $J=60$ periods. 

We set the coefficient of relative risk aversion to $\sigma=2$, and assign the equivalent household size by age cohort, $\{\xi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}$, using data from the 2016 Census. We scale household sizes by their square root, following standard practice in the literature. We determine the discount factor $\beta$, and the utility weight on housing services $\gamma$, in the second stage of our calibration.

We adopt the labor productivity process outlined in \citet{gkkoc}. The standard deviation of the fixed effect is set to $0.128$, and the stochastic labor productivity shocks follow an AR(1) process in logarithmic terms,

\begin{equation*}
  \log e' = \rho_{e} \log e + \epsilon_{e},\quad \epsilon_{e}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}} \sim N(0,\sigma_{e}).
\end{equation*}

\noindent We set the persistence parameter $\rho_{e}$ to 0.9, following \citet{gkkoc}. The standard deviation of the stochastic component of labor productivity $\sigma_{e}$, is calibrated internally. Newborns draw their stochastic productivity from the unconditional probability distribution associated with the ergodic distribution, which is endogenously generated by our productivity process. Consistent with \citet{az-2024}, we modify the stochastic process for homeless households by increasing by 20\% the probability of moving to or remaining in the lowest productivity state.  

We use 2016 Census data to estimate the deterministic life-cycle productivity component as:

\begin{equation*}
\zeta_j = 0.923+0.09\times j-1.61\times 10^{-3}\times j^{2}, \mbox{ for } j\leq J_{R},
\end{equation*}

\noindent where the coefficients are obtained from a regression of average household income for each age, ranging from 26 to 64, on age and age squared. 

We determine pension benefits ${y_{R}(\chi,e)}$, based on the replacement rate of gross individual earnings in Canada estimated by \citet{OECD-pension}. Surviving households' endowments are determined proportionally to their labor income, expressed as $\vartheta(\chi,e):=\vartheta \chi e$. We select the parameter $\vartheta$, governing surviving households' wealth endowments, to fulfill equation \eqref{market_clearing:agg_bequests}.

\subsubsection{Taxes and interest rates}

We set the taxable portion of capital income to $\nu=0.5$, consistent with the Canadian tax code. Utilizing household-level data from the 2016 Census for Toronto, we estimate the parameter $\psi=0.9275$ in equation \eqref{eq:income_tax}, which determines the progressivity of income taxes. We set the consumption tax rate $\tau_{c}$ to 13\% to reflect the Harmonized Sales Tax (HST) in Ontario. The interest rate is set to $r=2.77\%$, following the estimate for Canada by \citet{bruneau-etal-2018}.

\subsubsection{Housing}

We set the per-household public cost of homelessness to $\theta_{b}=82.3\%$ of average household income. We determine this parameter by dividing the City of Toronto's 2021 total spending on homelessness supports by the number of homeless individuals.\footnote{See \url{https://www.homelesshub.ca/community-profile/toronto} and \url{https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2021/ex/bgrd/backgroundfile-163804.pdf}. In the vast majority of cases, individuals experiencing homelessness are single adults. As a result, using individual-level data for homelessness spending is effectively equivalent to using household-level data.}

We set the depreciation rate of housing $\delta=0.6\%$ based on estimates from \citet{rosenthal-2014}. The decline in services per year of tenancy, due to landlords' lack of maintenance of controlled units, is therefore set to $\kappa_{2}=0.6\%$. This aligns with estimates from \citet{murray_et_al-1991}, who analyze a rent control regime similar to our model with tenancy control.\footnote{In their Table I, they estimate that unit deterioration of controlled units ranges from 2.2\% to 6.9\% over an eight-year period, implying an average annual deterioration rate of almost 0.6\%.}

The property tax rate is set to $\tau_p=0.44\%$, based on Altus Group reports and Property Sale and Assessment Data (PSAD) from 1992 to 2016. Following \citet{gm-2003}, we set buying costs to $\tau_{b}=2\%$ of the purchase price, and selling costs to $\tau_{s}=6\% $ of the sale price. We set renters’ moving costs, $\tau_r$, to 2.3\% of average labor income in the benchmark, corresponding to \$2,500 in 2016. This parameter captures expenses related to searching for a new unit, packing, transportation, and hiring movers. For homeless individuals, it also reflects additional barriers, including lower acceptance probabilities due to perceived homelessness and the difficulty of affording upfront large payments such as first and last months’ rent.

To target a price elasticity of housing supply of 0.89 as estimated for Toronto by \citet{piaxao-2021}, we set the cost curvature parameter $\varsigma_{2}=1.89$. The construction company's cost function level, denoted as $\varsigma_1$, is a free parameter that we derive from the market clearing condition \eqref{eq:housing_clearing_condition}.

We pick $\theta_1$, which determines the free-market rent, to generate a free-market rent to house price ratio of 5.82\%, based on our analyses utilizing 2016 Census and 2016 CREA data. We share our full calculation in the Appendix (\ref{appendix:data}).

We calibrate $\phi=0.468$ using 2016 Census data for Toronto, Calgary, and Edmonton. Specifically, we calculate the rental utilization rates for condominiums and single-family homes in Calgary and Edmonton, two major Canadian cities without rent control. The averages of each type-specific rates are then combined using Toronto’s actual distribution of housing types to obtain the final estimate. 

Furthermore, we set the parameter $\theta_2$, which governs the convexity of the marginal management cost in the rental housing sector and determines the rental supply elasticity, to 3.71. When $\theta_2=1$, the marginal management cost is constant, implying perfectly elastic rental supply. As $\theta_2$ increases above one, the marginal management cost rises more steeply with the scale of rental supply, reducing supply elasticity and increasing the cost of managing additional units. The value we pick for $\theta_2$ generates a rental supply elasticity that is equal to 1 in our benchmark. This value is somewhat lower than the 1.6 estimate for the overall U.S. housing market reported by \citet{rs-2023}, but it is consistent with evidence that supply elasticities are substantially lower in dense urban areas \citep{cy-1994,susin-2002,fack-2006,sy-2020}, which are the very ones where government normally considers rent control. Thus, we see our choice of elasticity of 1 as one that favors the case for decontrol over rent control.

\citet{zhang-gou} estimate that the average controlled rent is 13.7\% lower than free-market rents, holding other factors constant. According to the 2016 Census, about 30\% of the rental stock consisted of free-market units. Given our supply elasticity of 1 at the equilibrium quantity, this implies that the marginal cost of supplying the last controlled unit is approximately $\frac{0.3}{1}=0.3$ (or 30\%) lower than the free-market rent. Since this marginal cost is still below the controlled rent itself, the rental company has no incentive to withdraw units in our benchmark, and thus the total quantity of rental housing remains at the competitive level. Thus, in our benchmark, rent control does not reduce the overall supply of rental housing.

%We set the parameter $\epsilon_1$, which governs the extent to which the rent gap raises the probability of eviction, to 0.005. This value matches an estimated 0.1\% eviction rate in Toronto attributable to reasons consistent with this mechanism, such as noise, damage, landlord’s own use, renovation, and demolition \citep{LeonIveniuk2020}. 

Lastly, the initial rent charged to new tenants in tenancy-controlled units $\tilde{p}_{r,0}$, is also set externally to match the observed rent premium for new tenants relative to free-market rents. Specifically, we choose $\tilde{p}_{r,0}$ so that new tenants in controlled units pay 5.9\% above free-market rents, consistent with our point estimate for Toronto (95\% confidence interval: 2.7\%–9.1\%), as detailed in Section~\ref{section:appendix_measuring_premium} of the Appendix.

\subsubsection{Mortgages}

In accordance with banking regulations in Canada, we set the LTV constraint, denoted as $\lambda_{m,1}(ph)$, as follows:

\begin{equation*}
\lambda_{m,1}(ph) = \begin{cases}
0.8 &\text{if $ph\geq \$1M$}\\
0.9 &\text{if $\$500K\leq ph< \$1M$}\\
0.95 &\text{if $ph < \$500K$}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}

\noindent Households purchasing a home valued above a model-equivalent of \$1 million can borrow up to 80\% of its value; those purchasing homes valued between \$500,000 and \$1 million can borrow up to 90\% of the house value; and those purchasing homes valued below \$500,000 face a 95\% LTV cap. We set the PTI constraint parameter to $\lambda_{m,2}=0.39$, consistent with the 39\% PTI threshold commonly used in the Canadian mortgage industry.\footnote{See \url{https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/consumers/home-buying/calculators/debt-service-calculator}.}

Mortgage origination costs are set at a fixed amount equal to $\omega_{1}=0.68\%$ of the average household income in Toronto. This figure is based on the 2016 Census data and the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation's (CMHC) website, which includes standard expenses such as appraisal fees ($\approx$\$250) and lawyer fees ($\approx$\$500).\footnote{See \url{https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/en/professionals/industry-innovation-and-leadership/industry-expertise/resources-for-mortgage-professionals/how-much-will-my-home-really-cost}.}

The mortgage prepayment penalty, $\omega_{2}$, is set to three months of interest, a standard practice in Canadian mortgage contracts. Following the methodology in \citet{kkk-2021}, we set $\upsilon_{1}=1.7\%$, which reflects the average annual principal repayment rate under a 30-year amortization schedule. The threshold for incurring a prepayment penalty $\upsilon_{2}$, is set to $1.15 \times \upsilon_{1}$ plus 15\% of the annual mortgage payment amount (including both principal and interest), yielding an effective threshold of 18.8\%. This calibration mirrors contractual terms in Canada, which typically allow borrowers to increase their annual mortgage payments by up to 15\% and make lump-sum payments of up to 15\% of the original principal without penalty.

Finally, we set the interest rate premium on mortgage debt, $\Delta_{m}$, to 0.19\%. This value corresponds to the historical average spread between 5-year variable mortgage rates and 10-year Government of Canada bond yields over the 2002-2022 period.\footnote{While the mortgage rate data we use are not publicly available, historical 10-year bond yields can be accessed at \url{https://www.bankofcanada.ca/rates/interest-rates/canadian-bonds/}.}

\subsection{Internally calibrated parameters}

After assigning the externally determined parameter values discussed above, we proceed to calibrate another twelve parameters, as outlined in Table \ref{tab:calibration_internal}. These variables include the discount factor, $\beta$; the utility weight on the consumption of housing services, $\gamma$; the strength of the bequest motive, $\mu$; the average income taxation level, $\tau_{\ell}$; the variability in labor income's stochastic component, $\sigma_{e}$; the per-period rent decline for continued tenancy, $\kappa_1$; the parameter governing how strongly the rent gap affects eviction risk, $\epsilon_1$; the parameter governing the reduction in the probability of moving to or remaining in high labor productivity states for controlled tenants, $\kappa_3$; the parameter that determines how strongly this effect impacts high-income relative to low-income renters, $\kappa_4$; the minimum housing unit size for purchase, $\underline{h}_{o}$; the smallest rental, $\underline{h}_{r}$; and housing services for homeless households, $\underline{h}_{h}$.\footnote{The construction cost scalar, $\varsigma{1}$, is a free parameter selected to equate housing supply to housing demand in the benchmark equilibrium. This value remains fixed in all counterfactual simulations.}

We collectively calibrate these parameters to match twelve key empirical moments in our Toronto data:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The ratio of aggregate mortgage debt to aggregate house values among homeowners, which stands at 21.5\% according to the 2019 Survey of Financial Security (SFS).
    \item The average rent to income ratio, which we estimate at 31.1\% using the 2016 Census data.
    \item The ratio of aggregate bequests to aggregate net wealth, which is 2.2\%, based on estimates we derive from the 2019 SFS.
    \item The average labor income tax rate, recorded at 19.6\%, from the 2016 Census data.
    \item The Gini coefficient of labor income, which we also obtain from the 2016 Census.
    \item The ratio of average controlled rent to free-market rent, which is 0.863 based on estimates by \citet{zhang-gou} for Toronto.
    \item The percentage of renters evicted because landlords can obtain higher rents from new tenants, estimated at 0.1\% based on Toronto data from \citet{LeonIveniuk2020}.
    \item The percentage of renters in the controlled market, which we estimate is 71\% in the 2016 Census.
    \item The ratio of the average income of renters in the free market to that of renters in the controlled market, which we estimate is 1.19 using 2016 Census data. %The percentage of households in the top income quintile who rent in the controlled market, estimated at 4.1\% based on 2016 Census data.
    \item The homeownership rate, which is 66.5\% in the 2016 Census.
    \item The percentage of renters spending over 50\% of their pre-tax income on rent, standing at 15.4\% according to the 2016 Census.
    \item The percentage of households experiencing homelessness, which is documented at 0.26\% according to the ``Homeless Hub".\footnote{See \url{https://www.homelesshub.ca/community-profile/toronto}.}
\end{itemize}

While all parameters are determined jointly in calibration, each one tends to exert a more significant influence on specific outcomes. Here is how:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discount factor, $\beta$: Primarily shapes homeowners' debt levels; lower $\beta$ values lead to higher homeowner debt levels.
    \item Utility weight on housing, $\gamma$: Influences the proportion of renters' income spent on housing; higher $\gamma$ values indicate greater desire for housing services among households.
    \item Bequest motive strength, $\mu$: Affects the ratio of bequests to net wealth; higher $\mu$ values result in relatively more bequests compared to overall net wealth.
    \item Average income taxation level, $\tau_{\ell}$: Determines the average income tax rate; lower $\tau_{\ell}$ values correspond to higher tax burdens on households' incomes.
    \item Variability in labor income's stochastic component, $\sigma_{e}$: Shapes labor income inequality; higher $\sigma_{e}$ values lead to greater dispersion in the labor income distribution and thus higher Gini coefficients.
    \item Annual rent decrease for each additional period a tenant remains in the unit, $\kappa_1$: Governs how much rents decline over time for sitting tenants. Higher values of $\kappa_1$ result in a lower average controlled rent relative to the free-market rent.    
    \item Increased risk of eviction due to a larger rent gap, $\epsilon_1$: Determines how strongly the rent gap increases the probability of eviction under tenancy control. Higher values of $\epsilon_1$ lead to a higher eviction rate attributable to landlords seeking higher rents from new tenants.
    \item Reduction in the probability of transitioning to higher income states or remaining in high income states due to rent discounts, $\kappa_3$: Affects the likelihood that renters transition to lower-income states when renting in the controlled market and obtaining larger discounts. Larger values of $\kappa_3$ decrease the share of renters who choose to remain in the controlled segment.
    \item Differential impact on transitions to lower income states by income level, $\kappa_4$: Modulates how strongly the reduction in upward mobility affects high-income versus low-income renters. Higher values of $\kappa_4$ imply that higher-income renters face a greater penalty, reducing their incentive to rent in the controlled market.
    \item Minimum housing unit size for purchase, $\underline{h}_{o}$: Influences the homeownership rate; larger minimum sizes lead to fewer households opting for homeownership.
    \item Minimum rental size, $\underline{h}_{r}$: Governs the proportion of renters spending over 50\% of their pre-tax income on housing; larger minimum sizes result in higher percentages of renters facing high housing cost burdens. As demonstrated by \citet{rs-2023}, this parameter plays a crucial role in matching the observed distribution of rent-to-income ratios and, consequently, in determining welfare outcomes.
    \item Housing services for homeless households, $\underline{h}_{h}$: Affects the homelessness rate; greater services result in higher rates of homelessness.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model performance}\label{sec:non_targeted_statistics}

We assess the performance of our benchmark model by evaluating its ability to replicate aggregate, lifecycle, and distributional moments not targeted in our calibration. Table \ref{tab:aggregate_non_targreted_statistics} reports the model's fit relative to key non-targeted aggregate statistics. The model generates a similar ratio of average owned-house value to average household income compared to the data, alongside a somewhat lower percentage of owners with positive mortgage debt. Due to data limitations, we cannot directly estimate the ratio of average rental unit value to average owner-occupied unit value in Toronto. Hence, we compare our model's moment with the U.S. estimate reported by \citet{chatterjee-eyigungor-2015}, finding similar ratios. Lastly, we note that since approximately 30\% of renters in our benchmark reside in the free market and the utilization rate of the exempt stock is $\phi=46.8\%$, about 21.9\% of the total housing stock in our benchmark is exempt from rent control.

%Lastly, free-market renters in our model earn approximately 29\% more than controlled renters, closely matching the gap observed in the 2016 Census data. This suggests that the model generates a reasonable degree of income-based sorting between the rent-controlled and free-market segments.

The model's wealth distribution is quite unequal with a Gini coefficient of 0.47, though lower than the 0.65 observed in our 2019 Survey of Financial Security (SFS) data. The model understates wealth concentration at the top relative to the data due to the absence of rate-of-return heterogeneity, which \citet{fagereng} identify as a key determinant of upper-tail wealth accumulation. Our model also omits heterogeneous time preferences \citep{ks-1998} and the type of fat-tailed income shocks studied in \citet{mj-shocks}, both of which are known to amplify wealth inequality.

The model generates lifecycle patterns in homeownership rates and LTV ratios that resemble those observed in the data. Panel (a) in Figure \ref{fig:life_cycle} shows similar patterns in homeownership rates, though the model slightly over-predicts homeownership among middle-aged households and under-predicts it for young cohorts. Panel (b) plots LTV ratios, which decline with age in both the data and the model. Young households in the model hold higher LTV ratios than observed in the data, whereas older households exhibit somewhat lower leverage.

Figure \ref{fig:rent_to_income_cdf} displays the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of rent-to-income ratios. Our model replicates the observed distribution well, though it somewhat underestimates the proportion of renters with high rent-to-income ratios and somewhat overestimates the share of renters with low ratios.

Table \ref{tab:tenure_by_income_quintile} reports the shares of households living in the controlled market by income quintile. The second column from the left represents the lowest income quintile, while the first column from the right corresponds to the highest quintile. The first row reports statistics from the 2016 Census, while the second row shows the corresponding estimates in the calibrated model. The model closely replicates the empirical distribution across income quintiles.

Overall, the model closely replicates both key features observed in the data. It particularly does well on the rent-to-income CDF and the shares of households renting in the controlled market across income quintiles. Combined, this lends more credibility to the subsequent policy experiments that assess the welfare implications of alternative rent control regimes.

\section{Experiments}\label{section:experiments}

Using our calibrated model, we conduct several experiments to quantify how various rental-market policies affect metrics such as newborns' welfare, and equilibrium rents, housing prices, rental supply, homeownership, and homelessness. In all of our experiments we compare the different alternative policies to our calibrated benchmark. We present the results of our experiments in Table \ref{tab:aggregate_effects}.

In all experiments, the local government budget is balanced by adjusting the property tax rate. Changes in landlord profits are rebated as lump-sum transfers to households in the top 35\% of the wealth distribution. These transfers are then added to each household's taxable income and taxed by the federal government. The resulting excess federal revenues are redistributed either through income tax cuts to all households (Experiments 1–3) or as lump-sum transfers to renters and homeless households only (Experiment 4).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Experiment 1:} We compare the benchmark equilibrium — in which tenancy control coexists with a free rental market and partial exemptions — to a scenario of \textit{full decontrol}, where no rental units are regulated. This experiment evaluates whether the redistributive benefits of tenancy control outweigh its distortions to rental supply, the allocation of housing, labor productivity, and eviction.
    \item \textbf{Experiment 2:} We modify the tenancy control regime to remove its redistributive component while ensuring that rent discounts match the deterioration in housing services, potentially enabling renters to better optimize their housing services consumption and housing spending over time.
    \item \textbf{Experiment 3:} We contrast \textit{unit control} with our benchmark, in which tenancy control serves as a redistributive tool by providing substantial rent discounts relative to free-market rents that accumulate over time. This experiment allows us to assess whether unit control can outperform full decontrol, given that the magnitudes of its distortionary effects may differ from those of tenancy control. For example, because landlords cannot raise rents for new tenants under unit control, there is no eviction risk to sitting tenants. Moreover, since unit control offers large rent discounts immediately — rather than gradually — tenants have weaker incentives to remain in the unit long term. As a result, labor productivity is more distorted early in the tenancy but less so over time.
    \item \textbf{Experiment 4:} In our fourth experiment, we repeat Experiments 1--3 but rebate excess government revenues as lump-sum transfers to renters and the homeless instead of through income tax cuts. This experiment highlights that when redistribution is well-targeted, full decontrol would outperform both unit control and tenancy control regimes in which the main objective is redistribution. However, when tenancy control is used solely to mitigate housing over-consumption — and does not introduce substantial distortions — full decontrol would still underperform.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Welfare measure} Our welfare metric is the expected ex-ante utility of newborns \citep{ralws-1971,conesa-et-al,gkkoc}. This measure is conceptually equivalent to providing each newborn with a fixed proportional transfer of non-housing consumption. Formally, the non-housing consumption-equivalent welfare effect of a policy change is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
  CE = \left[\frac{\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left(V^{\dagger}_{1}(s)-\omega_{B}\left(q'^{\star}_{J}(s)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\Psi^{\dagger}_{1}(s)}{\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left(V^{\star}_{1}(s)-\omega_{B}\left(q'^{\star}_{J}(s)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\Psi^{\star}_{1}(s)}\right]^{\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)(1-\sigma)}}-1,
\end{equation}

\noindent where daggers denote objects in the counterfactual equilibrium, and stars refer benchmark objects. 

\subsection{Experiment 1}\label{sec:exp_1}

In our first experiment, we fully decontrol the rental market and assess the resulting effects on welfare and other key outcomes, as shown in the first row of Table \ref{tab:aggregate_effects}. This exercise evaluates whether the redistributive benefits of tenancy control outweigh the distortions it generates, in the absence of efficient government redistribution of landlord profits.

Removing tenancy control would increase landlord profits, resulting in lump-sum transfers equal to 0.85\% of average income for each household in the top 35\% of the wealth distribution. These transfers are added to taxable income and taxed at each household’s marginal rate, generating enough revenue to finance a 0.87\% reduction in average income taxes.

Because the incentive to remain in a rental unit and accumulate rent discounts would disappear, more households would transition to homeownership, increasing the homeownership rate by 2.44 percentage points (p.p.). This would raise housing demand and push house prices up by 2.19\%. Meanwhile, reduced demand for rental housing would cause free-market rents to fall by 2.17\%, while the equilibrium rental quantity would decline by 3.24\%. However, average rents would rise relative to the benchmark because free-market rents would decline only somewhat, while the previously deeply discounted controlled rents would be eliminated.

The loss of tenure-dependent rent discounts would also weaken renters’ ability to weather severe income shocks, leading to a 4.28\% increase in homelessness.

Nonetheless, newborn welfare would rise by 0.77\%, as the removal of tenancy control would lead to income tax cuts, extra lump-sum transfers to the top of the wealth distribution, and the elimination of key distortions: misallocation of housing, reduced labor productivity among controlled renters, and increased eviction risk. Thus, even though tenancy control partially redistributes resources from landlords to low-income renters, the costs and distortions it introduces outweigh its redistributive benefits.

\subsection{Experiment 2}\label{sec:exp_2}

Given that our benchmark tenancy control regime — designed to redistribute resources — fails to improve welfare relative to full decontrol, our next experiment evaluates whether tenancy control could enhance welfare under a modified configuration.

In the presence of binding minimum rental size constraints, many low-income renters are compelled to consume more housing services than they prefer. This raises the possibility that a tenancy control regime designed not to redistribute, but simply to reduce rent at the same rate as landlord neglect, could benefit renters. On the one hand, such a regime would lower both housing expenditures and the over-consumption of housing services for new tenants. On the other hand, it may hurt those whose housing services fall below their optimum, yet face fixed moving costs that are high relative to their incomes and would therefore remain in suboptimal rentals.

To evaluate which channel dominates, we modify the tenancy control regime so that the annual rent discount $\kappa_1$ exactly offsets the rate of housing services deterioration $\delta$. This policy setup reduces the distortions associated with tenancy control while addressing an important friction in housing consumption. 

Under this policy setup, tenancy control reduces only the nominal rent paid by controlled renters, not their rent after adjusting for housing services. If renters benefit from this arrangement, they must be willing to pay a premium above free-market rents to access controlled units. Since these rents do not fall in real terms, they always exceed free-market levels. Therefore, landlords would prefer to only offer controlled rentals, which have higher returns. As a result, the entire rental market would effectively fall under control.

This policy design would eliminate the distortions that arise when tenancy control is used as a redistributive tool. As a result, as shown in the second row of Table \ref{tab:aggregate_effects}, most outcomes in this experiment would closely resemble those under full decontrol, with two key exceptions: homelessness and welfare. Since remaining in the same unit would not lead to rent reductions after adjusting for housing services, more households would choose homeownership, increasing the homeownership rate by 1.8 p.p. This shift would raise house prices by 0.94\% and reduce rental demand, which in turn would lower initial controlled rents by 10.47\%. In addition, the higher profits earned by the rental and construction firms would generate lump-sum transfers to the top 35\% of the wealth distribution equal to 0.26\% of the average income. After taxation, these transfers would fund a 0.26\% reduction in the average income tax rate.

Unlike the full decontrol case, however, the decline in nominal rents over a tenancy would reduce renters’ housing expenditures. Combined with the drop in initial controlled rents driven by general-equilibrium effects, this would enhance renters’ ability to afford housing during adverse income shocks, reducing homelessness by nearly half and ultimately raising welfare by 1.79\% relative to the benchmark, which is also 1.02\% higher than full decontrol.

\subsection{Experiment 3}\label{sec:exp_3}

In this experiment, we assess whether unit control — which would grant immediate rent discounts to households lucky enough to secure a controlled unit — could enhance welfare relative to both full decontrol and our benchmark tenancy control regime, where rent control is used as a redistributive tool.

We maintain the same share of exempt rental stock as in the benchmark (21.9\%) and set the immediate rent discount under unit control equal to the average discount accrued under tenancy control — approximately 13.7\% below the benchmark free-market rent and 19.6\% lower than the controlled rent new movers pay in the tenancy control benchmark. 

Because unit control prevents initial rents from adjusting to clear the controlled market, we set the probability of securing a controlled unit ($\eta=1$) at $\pi=8.4\%$ to equate the demand for controlled rentals with their supply. Although the chance of drawing $\eta=1$ is i.i.d. across households and time, the decision to enter the controlled market — and how long to remain there — is still endogenous. As a result, even under unit control, the allocation of controlled units would be far from random. As shown in the third row of Table \ref{tab:tenure_by_income_quintile}, fewer controlled units would go to the poorest households relative to the benchmark, though the distribution would still favor lower-income households over higher-income ones.

Since unit control provides a fixed discount that does not increase with tenure, more households would opt for homeownership. This shift would slightly raise house prices and reduce rental demand, leading to a 0.86\% decline in free-market rents.

From the perspective of newborns, entering a lottery with a chance of receiving a large immediate discount would be more appealing than paying a premium to access a tenancy-controlled unit and waiting for rent discounts to accumulate. These deeper upfront discounts would also reduce homelessness by 16.93\%. The smaller reduction in homelessness relative to Experiment 2 is due to the lottery system under unit control, which excludes some low-income households who would have preferred to live in a controlled unit but did not win a spot. In contrast, because prices clear the controlled market under the tenancy control regime in Experiment 2, any household willing to pay the market-clearing rent for new tenants can access a controlled unit.

On the bright side, however, since landlords cannot raise rents for new tenants under unit control, long-term tenants face no eviction risk. Although labor productivity is more distorted early in a tenancy — due to the large immediate rent discounts — it becomes less distorted for long-term renters compared to tenancy control, where distortions grow as rent discounts accumulate with tenure.

Overall, welfare would rise by 0.99\% — exceeding even the gain from full decontrol. In Experiment 4, we show that unit control only outperforms full decontrol when landlord profits are redistributed inefficiently — namely, through income tax cuts that disproportionately benefit high-income households. When redistribution is instead targeted to renters and homeless households, full decontrol would outperform a unit control regime primarily designed to redistribute.

\subsection{Experiment 4}\label{sec:exp_4}

In our final experiment, we revisit Experiments 1-3, but instead of using excess government revenues to fund income tax cuts — which primarily benefit high-income households — we redistribute these revenues as lump-sum transfers to renters and the homeless, who tend to have lower incomes and net worth than homeowners.

The results, shown in rows 4–6 of Table \ref{tab:aggregate_effects}, indicate that the same mechanisms observed in Experiments 1–3 would still be at play. However, altering how the government redistributes excess revenues increases the incentive to rent, thereby reducing homeownership while raising both rental demand and equilibrium rents relative to the scenarios with income tax cuts — except in the unit control experiment, where renters and the homeless actually face lump-sum taxes rather than transfers due to a decline in free-market rents and, consequently, in landlord profits relative to the benchmark. As a result, welfare in this unit control scenario would fall compared to the case where excess revenues are redistributed through income tax cuts.

Importantly, these experiments highlight two key insights:
\begin{itemize}
  \item When excess government revenues are well-targeted, neither unit control nor tenancy control regimes aimed mostly at redistribution would outperform full decontrol. In our model, full decontrol would raise welfare by 1.24\% relative to the benchmark, whereas unit control would yield a smaller welfare gain of 0.90\%.
  \item When tenancy control is designed solely to reduce over-consumption of housing services, it avoids the distortions associated with redistribution-oriented policies. Since over-consumption is a friction inherent in full decontrol, addressing it directly — without introducing additional distortions — would raise welfare relative to full decontrol, regardless of how revenues are redistributed. In this counterfactual, welfare would rise by 0.64\% relative to full decontrol and 1.88\% relative to the benchmark.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We build a dynamic general equilibrium model, calibrate it to Toronto data, and show that when government redistributes landlord profits through income tax cuts, the resulting distortions of tenancy control outweigh its redistributive benefits. Because unit control provides immediate rent discounts, it can still raise welfare relative to full decontrol under such inefficient redistribution. However, when government revenue is instead targeted to renters and the homeless, even unit control performs worse than decontrol. In contrast, tenancy control can substantially increase welfare relative to decontrol when used specifically to curb over-consumption of housing services.

Our findings suggest that the central question is not “Should we have rent control?” but rather “How much, and what kind, of rent control should we have?” In particular, tenancy control — which allows prices to adjust to clear markets — emerges as the most effective regime for addressing over-consumption of housing services. By contrast, unit control requires the government to set rents directly. This increases the risk that rents will be set too low, leading to shortages and misallocation. As a result, rent control could easily once again inadvertently become a tool for redistribution — a role we have shown to be welfare-reducing.

In practice, a tenancy control system aimed at curbing housing over-consumption could be implemented by linking rent growth in the controlled sector to long-run trends in either free-market rents or initial controlled rents. Specifically, when a free-market rental sector exists, governments could allow landlords to raise controlled rents annually at the average long-run growth rate of free-market rents, net of depreciation. In fully controlled markets where free-market rent data naturally do not exist, this growth rate could instead be based on the historical trend in the growth rate of initial rents of newly controlled renters. This policy design preserves the benefit of rent control of limiting over-consumption while minimizing distortions to rental supply, allocation efficiency, labor productivity, and eviction risk.
